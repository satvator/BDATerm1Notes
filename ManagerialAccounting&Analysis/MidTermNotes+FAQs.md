# PART 1: FOUNDATIONS OF ACCOUNTING

## 1.1 What is Accounting?

Accounting is the **language of business**. It is an information system that:
1. **Measures** business activities,
2. **Processes** that information into reports, and
3. **Communicates** the results to decision-makers.

It helps answer questions like: How well is the business performing? Is it financially sound? Can it pay its loans? Is a customer/product profitable?

**Users of accounting information:**
- **External decision-makers** (owners, investors, lenders, government, customers) → rely on **Financial Accounting & Reporting** — summarized, standardized information available to everyone.
- **Internal decision-makers** (managers) → rely on **Management Accounting** — detailed, often confidential, decision-specific information available only inside the company.

## 1.2 The Four Main Financial Statements

| Statement | What it shows | Key equation |
|---|---|---|
| **Balance Sheet** | Financial position at *one point in time* | Assets = Liabilities + Equity |
| **Income Statement** | Performance *over a period* | Revenues − Expenses = Profit |
| **Cash Flow Statement** | Cash movement *over a period*, split into Operating / Investing / Financing | — |
| **Statement of Changes in Equity** | Changes in owner's equity over a period | — |

## 1.3 Key Accounting Principles (the "rules of the game")

- **Economic Entity Principle:** The business is a separate unit from its owners, even if it's a sole proprietorship.
- **Dual Aspect (Double-Entry) Principle:** Every transaction affects *two* accounts. Example: You borrow ₹5,000 from a friend → Cash increases by ₹5,000 AND a liability (amount owed) increases by ₹5,000.
- **Monetary Unit Assumption:** Only transactions expressible in money are recorded.
- **Cost Principle:** Assets are recorded at their actual (historical) purchase cost, not their current market value.
- **Going Concern Assumption:** We assume the business will continue operating indefinitely (not about to shut down).
- **Consistency Principle:** Once an accounting policy is chosen, it should be applied consistently period after period, so statements remain comparable.
- **Periodicity Concept:** Even though a business runs indefinitely, we report results in shorter, fixed periods (annually/quarterly/monthly) to enable decisions.
- **Accrual Concept:** Revenue is recorded when it is *earned* (goods/service delivered), and expenses when *incurred* — regardless of when cash actually changes hands. (Required by Sec. 128 of the Companies Act, 2013 in India.)
- **Matching Concept:** Expenses are recorded in the *same period* as the revenue they helped generate.
  - *Product costs* (raw material, labour) are matched to the units sold.
  - *Period costs* (rent, salaries) are matched to the period in which they're incurred.
- **Conservatism:** Recognize expected *losses* as soon as there's a reasonable chance of them, but recognize *gains* only when actually realized.

# PART 2: THE ACCOUNTING EQUATION

## 2.1 The Basic Equation

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Owner's Equity}$$

- **Assets** = economic resources the business owns/controls that will provide future benefit (Cash, Inventory, Receivables, Furniture, Land, etc.)
- **Liabilities** = what the business owes to outsiders (loans, trade payables, taxes payable)
- **Owner's Equity** = the owners' claim on the business (their investment + accumulated profits)

Think of it as: **Resources = Claims on Resources.** Every rupee of assets was *financed* by either a creditor (liability) or an owner (equity) — nothing appears from nowhere.

### Assets: Current vs Non-current
- **Current Assets:** expected to convert to cash within 1 year (or one operating cycle) — Cash, Inventory, Trade Receivables, Prepaid Expenses.
- **Non-current Assets:** longer-term — Property, Plant & Equipment (PPE), Intangibles, Long-term Investments.

### Liabilities: Current vs Non-current
- **Current Liabilities:** due within 1 year — Trade Payables, Accrued Expenses, Short-term borrowings.
- **Non-current Liabilities:** due beyond 1 year — Long-term loans, bonds payable.

## 2.2 The Expanded Accounting Equation

Once a business starts earning profit and paying dividends, Equity splits into two parts:

**First year of operations:**
$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \big[\text{Contributed Capital} + (\text{Revenue} - \text{Expenses} - \text{Dividends})\big]$$

**Subsequent years:**
$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \big[\text{Contributed Capital} + \text{Previous Retained Earnings} + (\text{Revenue} - \text{Expenses} - \text{Dividends})\big]$$

**Retained Earnings roll-forward:**
$$\text{Closing Retained Earnings} = \text{Opening Retained Earnings} + \text{Profit for the period} - \text{Dividends paid}$$

## 2.3 Worked Example — Building the Equation Transaction by Transaction

*Sheldon Cooper starts "HearWell," an electronics trading firm.*

| # | Transaction | Effect on equation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sheldon invests ₹10,00,000 cash | Cash (Asset) ↑ 10,00,000; Contributed Capital ↑ 10,00,000 |
| 2 | Buys a store for ₹40,00,000: pays ₹6,00,000 cash + ₹34,00,000 mortgage loan | Store (Asset) ↑ 40,00,000; Cash ↓ 6,00,000; Loan (Liability) ↑ 34,00,000 |
| 3 | Buys stock worth ₹5,00,000: pays ₹2,00,000 cash, balance on 1-month credit | Inventory (Asset) ↑ 5,00,000; Cash ↓ 2,00,000; Trade Payables (Liability) ↑ 3,00,000 |

**Check:** Assets = Cash (10,00,000 − 6,00,000 − 2,00,000 = 2,00,000) + Store (40,00,000) + Inventory (5,00,000) = **47,00,000**
Liabilities + Equity = Loan (34,00,000) + Trade Payables (3,00,000) + Capital (10,00,000) = **47,00,000** ✓ — the equation always balances!

### Continuing the example (with Income Statement effects)
| # | Transaction | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | Sells half the stock (cost ₹2,50,000) for ₹4,00,000 | Inventory ↓ 2,50,000; Cash (or Receivable) ↑ 4,00,000; Revenue ↑ 4,00,000; COGS (Expense) ↑ 2,50,000 → net effect on Equity (via profit) = +1,50,000 |
| 5 | Pays staff salaries ₹50,000 | Cash ↓ 50,000; Expense ↑ 50,000 → Equity (via profit) ↓ 50,000 |

Every revenue **increases** equity (through profit); every expense **decreases** equity (through profit) — that's why the expanded equation embeds (Revenue − Expenses) inside the Equity term.

# PART 3: TRANSACTION ANALYSIS

Real business questions rarely hand you a clean list of "assets and liabilities" — you often need to **back into** an unknown number (like Trade Receivables, Payables, or Inventory Purchases) using timing rules on cash collection/payment. Below are the core patterns, each explained with a full worked example.

## 3.1 Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)

$$\text{COGS} = \text{Opening Inventory} + \text{Purchases} - \text{Closing Inventory}$$

For manufacturers, "inventory" includes Raw Material + WIP + Finished Goods + Stock in Trade — all four must be valued at period-end.

**Worked Example:** AB Ltd. trades office chairs.
- May: bought 4,000 chairs @ ₹7,200 = ₹2,88,00,000 purchases. Sold 3,400 chairs @ ₹12,100.
- June: bought 4,200 chairs @ ₹7,400 = ₹3,10,80,000 purchases. Sold 4,100 chairs. *(All May chairs are sold by June-end.)*

**May COGS** = 3,400 chairs sold × ₹7,200 (May's cost) = **₹2,44,80,000**
(Opening inventory = 0; Closing inventory = 4,000 − 3,400 = 600 chairs × ₹7,200 = ₹43,20,000; check: 0 + 2,88,00,000 − 43,20,000 = 2,44,80,000 ✓)

**June COGS:** June opens with 600 May-chairs (₹7,200 each) + buys 4,200 June-chairs (₹7,400 each). It sells 4,100 chairs, and since "all May chairs are sold by June end," all 600 May-chairs go first, plus 3,500 June-chairs.
COGS = (600 × 7,200) + (3,500 × 7,400) = 43,20,000 + 2,59,00,000 = **₹3,02,20,000**

## 3.2 Trade Receivables — Timing of Collections

**Key idea:** Not all sales are collected in the same month they're made. Use a **collection schedule**: track what % of each month's sales is collected in that month, the next month, etc.

**Worked Example:** Green Company — sales are 20% cash, 80% credit. Of credit sales: 60% collected in month of sale, 30% in the following month, 10% two months later.
Sales: June $300,000; July $250,000; Aug $280,000; Sept $310,000.

Credit sales: June $240,000; July $200,000; Aug $224,000; Sept $248,000.

**Cash collected in August** = 
- Cash sales of Aug (20% × 280,000) = $56,000
- 60% of Aug credit sales collected in Aug = 0.60 × 224,000 = $134,400
- 30% of July credit sales collected in Aug (1 month later) = 0.30 × 200,000 = $60,000
- 10% of June credit sales collected in Aug (2 months later) = 0.10 × 240,000 = $24,000
- **Total Aug collections = 56,000 + 134,400 + 60,000 + 24,000 = $274,400**

**Trade Receivables at end of August** = uncollected credit sales still outstanding:
- From Aug credit sales: 40% still outstanding = 0.40 × 224,000 = $89,600
- From July credit sales: 10% still outstanding (only the 2-months-later portion) = 0.10 × 200,000 = $20,000
- **Total Receivables at end of Aug = $109,600**

*(Same logic extends to September — just roll the schedule forward one month.)*

## 3.3 Trade Payables — Timing of Payments (mirror logic)

**Worked Example:** Arjun's firm buys school supplies.
- May: purchases ₹30,00,000, paid ₹18,00,000 in May, balance payable later.
- June: purchases ₹38,00,000, 60% (₹22,80,000) paid in June; also pays ₹9,00,000 of the May purchases in June.

**Payment made in June** = ₹22,80,000 (for June purchases) + ₹9,00,000 (for May purchases) = **₹31,80,000**

**Trade Payables at end of June:**
- Opening payable from May = 30,00,000 − 18,00,000 = 12,00,000; less ₹9,00,000 paid in June = ₹3,00,000 still owed from May.
- June purchases unpaid = 38,00,000 − 22,80,000 = ₹15,20,000
- **Total Payables at end of June = 3,00,000 + 15,20,000 = ₹18,20,000**

## 3.4 Inventory Purchases (working backward from desired closing stock)

$$\text{Purchases} = \text{COGS} + \text{Desired Closing Inventory} - \text{Opening Inventory}$$

**Worked Example:** Walker Entertainment — COGS = 70% of sales. Ending inventory = 40% of *next month's* COGS.
Sales: Jan $100,000; Feb $150,000; Mar $180,000; Apr $200,000.

- Feb COGS = 0.70 × 150,000 = $105,000
- Feb closing inventory (= 40% of March's COGS) = 0.40 × (0.70 × 180,000) = 0.40 × 126,000 = $50,400
- Feb opening inventory (= Jan's closing inventory = 40% of Feb's COGS) = 0.40 × 105,000 = $42,000
- **February Purchases** = COGS + Closing Inv − Opening Inv = 105,000 + 50,400 − 42,000 = **$113,400**

- **Opening inventory for April** = closing inventory of March = 40% of April's COGS = 0.40 × (0.70 × 200,000) = 0.40 × 140,000 = **$56,000**

## 3.5 Putting it Together — A Mini Cash Budget

**Worked Example:** The Brady Company, April.
- Sales $1,000,000 (80% cash, 20% credit — credit collected 1 month later)
- March sales $900,000 (80% cash, 20% credit — credit collected in April)
- Inventory purchases $800,000, paid in cash
- S&A expenses $80,000, paid in cash
- Depreciation $14,000 *(non-cash — never include in a cash budget!)*
- Opening cash balance (March 31) = $45,000

**Cash inflows in April** = April cash sales (80% × 1,000,000 = $800,000) + March credit sales collected in April (20% × 900,000 = $180,000) = **$980,000**

**Cash outflows in April** = Inventory purchases $800,000 + S&A expenses $80,000 = **$880,000** *(depreciation is excluded — it's a non-cash accounting expense, not an actual cash outflow)*

**Closing cash balance** = 45,000 + 980,000 − 880,000 = **$145,000**

# PART 4: DEPRECIATION & NON-CURRENT ASSETS

## 4.1 What is Property, Plant & Equipment (PPE)?

Tangible items that are: (a) held for use in production/supply of goods or services, for rental, or admin purposes, and (b) expected to be used for **more than one period**. PPE is *not* meant to be sold in the normal course of business (unlike inventory).

## 4.2 What is Depreciation?

> "Depreciation is the systematic allocation of the depreciable amount of an asset over its useful life."

Depreciation happens due to: normal wear and tear, consumption through use, and the passage of time/obsolescence (technology or market changes).

**Important points:**
- Depreciation is a **cost allocation** process, *not* a valuation process — you still depreciate an asset even if its market value has risen.
- Depreciation **stops** once the asset is disposed of.
- **Land** is not depreciated (indefinite life) — but quarries/landfill sites *are* depreciated (limited life).

## 4.3 Step 1: Establishing the Depreciable Amount

$$\text{Depreciable Amount} = \text{Cost of Acquisition} - \text{Net Residual (Salvage) Value}$$

- **Cost of acquisition** = all costs to get the asset ready for its intended use.
- **Net residual value** = expected disposal proceeds *minus* estimated disposal costs.

**Example:** Machinery costs ₹4,00,000, expected residual ₹45,000, disposal costs ₹5,000.
Net residual value = 45,000 − 5,000 = ₹40,000
**Depreciable amount = 4,00,000 − 40,000 = ₹3,60,000**

## 4.4 Step 2: Estimating Useful Life

Useful life = the period an asset is expected to be usable by the business (OR the number of production units it's expected to yield) — this is often **shorter** than its physical life, due to: technological obsolescence, product/market changes, regulatory restrictions, voluntary replacement, or heavy use (e.g., double shifts).

**Example:** A mainframe computer could physically last 8 years, but management plans to replace it in 4 years once a superior model launches → **useful life for depreciation = 4 years**, not 8.

## 4.5 The Three Depreciation Methods

### (A) Straight-Line Method (SLM)
Spreads the depreciable amount **equally** across the useful life. Assumes depreciation is purely a function of time, not usage.

$$\text{Annual Depreciation} = \frac{\text{Cost} - \text{Residual Value}}{\text{Useful Life (years)}}$$

**Worked Example:** Machinery cost ₹8,00,000, residual ₹80,000, useful life 6 years.
Annual Depreciation = (8,00,000 − 80,000) / 6 = 7,20,000 / 6 = **₹1,20,000 per year** (same every year)

### (B) Written Down Value / Diminishing Balance Method (WDV)
Applies a **fixed percentage rate** to the asset's *carrying amount* (Net Book Value) at the start of each year — so depreciation is highest in year 1 and declines every year after.

$$\text{Depreciation Rate} = 1 - \sqrt[n]{\frac{\text{Residual Value}}{\text{Cost}}}$$

**Worked Example:** Asset costs ₹200,000, useful life 6 years, salvage ₹10,000.
Rate = 1 − (10,000/200,000)^(1/6) = 1 − (0.05)^(0.1667) ≈ 1 − 0.6021 ≈ **39.79%**

| Year | Opening NBV | Depreciation (39.79%) | Closing NBV |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2,00,000 | 79,580 | 1,20,420 |
| 2 | 1,20,420 | 47,923 | 72,497 |
| 3 | 72,497 | 28,847 | 43,650 |

**Accumulated depreciation after 3 years** = 79,580 + 47,923 + 28,847 = **₹1,56,350**

*Logic: WDV assumes repair costs rise as the asset ages, so higher depreciation early + lower repairs early "balances" against lower depreciation late + higher repairs late.*

### (C) Units of Production Method
Ties depreciation directly to **actual usage** (units made, hours run, km driven) rather than time.

$$\text{Depreciation for the year} = \text{Depreciable Amount} \times \frac{\text{Units produced this year}}{\text{Total estimated production capacity}}$$

**Worked Example:** Equipment costs ₹1,20,000, expected to produce 4,00,000 units total.
Year 1: 50,000 units produced → Depreciation = 1,20,000 × (50,000/4,00,000) = **₹15,000**
Year 2: 60,000 units → Depreciation = 1,20,000 × (60,000/4,00,000) = **₹18,000**
Year 3: 70,000 units → Depreciation = 1,20,000 × (70,000/4,00,000) = **₹21,000**
**Accumulated depreciation after 3 years = 15,000+18,000+21,000 = ₹54,000**

*(Note: if there's a residual value, use Depreciable Amount = Cost − Residual, not full cost, in the formula above.)*

### Quick Comparison
| Method | Best suited when… | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| SLM | Usage is fairly even over time (e.g., buildings, software, furniture) | Constant every year |
| WDV | Asset is more efficient/useful when new, and repair costs rise with age | High → low |
| Units of Production | Output/usage varies significantly year to year and can be measured (machine hours, km, units) | Matches actual usage |

## 4.6 Amortization (for Intangible Assets)

The portion of an **intangible asset** (software, patents, licences, trademarks) used up in the current year is called **Amortization** — conceptually identical to depreciation, but for intangibles, and usually computed on a straight-line basis.

**Worked Example:** A telecom company buys a 5G licence for ₹3,000 crore, useful life 20 years, no salvage value.
Annual Amortization = 3,000 / 20 = **₹150 crore per year**

## 4.7 Partial-Year Depreciation

If an asset becomes ready for use partway through the year, depreciation is charged **proportionately** from that date.

**Worked Example:** Hospital Management Software costs ₹18,00,000, no residual value, useful life 6 years, ready for use **1 October 2025** (FY runs Apr–Mar).
Full annual depreciation = 18,00,000 / 6 = ₹3,00,000
FY 2025–26 depreciation (6 months used, Oct–Mar) = 3,00,000 × 6/12 = **₹1,50,000**
FY 2026–27 onward = full ₹3,00,000/year.

# PART 5: CASH FLOW STATEMENT (CFS)

## 5.1 Why a Separate Statement for Cash?

The Income Statement and Balance Sheet are based on the **accrual concept** (revenue/expenses recorded when earned/incurred, not when cash moves). The CFS instead follows **cash accounting** — it shows the actual cash receipts and payments during the period.

> "Cash is the blood of the business — without it, a business cannot survive." Profit ≠ Cash!

## 5.2 The Three Classifications

| Category | Includes |
|---|---|
| **Operating Activities** | Day-to-day revenue-generating activities: cash from customers, cash paid to suppliers/employees, other operating expenses |
| **Investing Activities** | Buying/selling long-term assets: PPE, intangibles, investments in equity/debt securities |
| **Financing Activities** | Raising/repaying capital: issuing/repaying debt or equity, interest paid, dividends paid |

**First-level format:**
```
Cash Flow from Operating Activities        XXXX
Cash Flow from Investing Activities        XXXX
Cash Flow from Financing Activities        XXXX
Net Increase/Decrease in Cash              XXXX
Add: Cash balance at beginning of year     XXXX
Cash balance at end of year                XXXX
```

## 5.3 Cash Flow from Operating Activities — Indirect Method

Start from **Profit Before Tax** and adjust for non-cash items and working-capital changes:

```
Profit Before Tax                                     XXXX
Add: Depreciation & Amortisation (non-cash expense)    XXXX
Add: Non-operating expenses (interest, loss on sale)   XXXX
Less: Non-operating income (dividend/interest income,
       profit on sale of assets)                      (XXXX)
Add/(Less): Decrease/(Increase) in Current Assets      XXXX
Add/(Less): Increase/(Decrease) in Current Liabilities XXXX
= Cash Generated from Operations                       XXXX
Less: Income Tax Paid                                  (XXXX)
= Cash Flow from Operating Activities                  XXXX
```

**Why add back depreciation?** It reduced Profit Before Tax on paper, but *no cash actually left the business* — so we add it back to undo that non-cash deduction.

**Why adjust for working capital?**
- If Trade Receivables *increase*, it means some revenue was recorded (on accrual basis) but the cash hasn't been collected yet → **subtract** the increase.
- If Inventory *increases*, cash was spent to build stock, but it hasn't hit the P&L as an expense yet → **subtract** the increase.
- If Trade Payables *increase*, expenses were incurred but not yet paid in cash → **add** the increase (cash was "saved").

## 5.4 Worked Example — Full Indirect-Method CFS

**GreenTech Manufacturing Ltd.** (₹ Lakhs)

| Particulars | 31-Mar-26 | 31-Mar-25 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Net PPE | 4,500 | 4,100 | +400 (Gross PPE up 900; Accum. Dep. up 500) |
| Inventory | 980 | 860 | +120 |
| Trade Receivables | 1,240 | 1,380 | −140 |
| Prepaid Expenses | 70 | 50 | +20 |
| Trade Payables | 920 | 980 | −60 |
| Income Tax Payable | 140 | 130 | +10 |
| Other Current Liabilities | 580 | 270 | +310 |
| Long-term Borrowings | 1,100 | 1,400 | −300 |

P&L: Profit Before Tax = 1,650; Depreciation = 500; Finance Cost = 180; Income Tax Expense = 450.

**Step 1 — Operating Activities:**
```
Profit Before Tax                             1,650
Add: Depreciation                               500
Add: Finance costs (non-operating, added back)  180
Less: Increase in Inventory                    (120)
Add: Decrease in Trade Receivables              140
Less: Increase in Prepaid Expenses              (20)
Less: Decrease in Trade Payables                (60)
Add: Increase in Income Tax Payable              10
Add: Increase in Other Current Liabilities      310
= Cash Generated from Operations              2,590
Less: Income Tax Paid (450 expense − 10 ↑ payable) (440)
= Cash Flow from Operating Activities         2,150
```
*(Note: Finance cost is often shown separately as "interest paid" under Financing Activities as per Ind AS/company practice — some formats add it back here and deduct it again as a financing outflow; always follow the specific convention your course uses.)*

**Step 2 — Investing Activities:**
Gross PPE increased by 900 (6,400 − 5,500) → this is cash paid to purchase new PPE, assuming no PPE was sold.
Long-term Investments increased by 200 (700 − 500) → cash paid to invest.
```
Payment for Purchase of PPE                    (900)
Payment for Purchase of Investments            (200)
= Cash Flow from Investing Activities         (1,100)
```

**Step 3 — Financing Activities:**
Long-term Borrowings decreased by 300 → loan repaid.
Retained Earnings increased by 850 (2,850 − 2,000), but Profit After Tax = 1,200 (1,650 − 450) → implies Dividends Paid = 1,200 − 850 = 350.
```
Repayment of Long-term Borrowings              (300)
Interest Paid                                  (180)
Dividends Paid                                 (350)
= Cash Flow from Financing Activities          (830)
```

**Step 4 — Net Change & Verification:**
```
Net Increase in Cash = 2,150 − 1,100 − 830 = 220
Opening Cash (31-Mar-25)                        390
Closing Cash (31-Mar-26)                        610 ≈ matches Balance Sheet's 600 (rounding)
```

## 5.5 Classifying Transactions — Quick Practice

| Transaction | Classification |
|---|---|
| Paid cash for office supplies | Operating |
| Issued bonds payable for cash | Financing |
| Received dividends from an investment | Operating (per Ind AS, often shown as Operating or Investing depending on policy) |
| Paid cash interest on a loan | Operating (or Financing, per accounting policy chosen — must be consistent) |
| Collected accounts receivable | Operating |
| Paid salaries | Operating |
| Purchased a patent for cash | Investing |
| Paid cash for advertising | Operating |
| Received cash from sale of an old delivery truck | Investing |
| Paid cash for income taxes | Operating |

## 5.6 Analyzing a Cash Flow Statement — Key Questions to Ask
- What are the major sources and uses of cash?
- Is Operating Cash Flow positive and large enough to cover Investing Cash Flow (i.e., is the business self-funding its growth)?
- Is Operating Cash Flow higher or lower than Operating Income (EBIT)? (If cash flow is *much* lower than accounting profit, investigate working capital or earnings quality.)
- Are Investing cash flows going into productive PPE, or just financial investments?
- Is the company increasingly reliant on borrowing (rising Financing inflows every year)?

# PART 6: PROFITABILITY ANALYSIS (Margins)

## 6.1 The "Waterfall" from Revenue to Net Profit

```
Revenue
Less: Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
= Gross Profit
Less: Operating Expenses (excl. Depreciation & Amortisation)
= EBITDA (Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation & Amortisation)
Less: Depreciation & Amortisation
= EBIT (Earnings Before Interest & Tax) — also called "Operating Profit"
Less: Interest (Finance Costs)
Add/Less: Non-operating income/expenses
= Profit Before Tax (PBT)
Less: Tax
= Profit After Tax (PAT) — "Net Profit"
```

## 6.2 The Four Key Margins

| Margin | Formula | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **Gross Profit Margin** | Gross Profit / Revenue | Pricing power & direct production cost efficiency |
| **EBITDA Margin** | EBITDA / Revenue | Core operating efficiency, stripped of financing/tax/depreciation policy differences — good for comparing companies |
| **EBIT (Operating) Margin** | EBIT / Revenue | Operating profitability *after* accounting for asset usage (depreciation) |
| **Net Profit Margin** | PAT / Revenue | Bottom-line profitability after everything — financing, tax, all costs |

**Why EBITDA is useful for comparison:** It removes the effects of capital structure (interest expense), tax jurisdiction/policy, and depreciation method choices — making it easier to compare operational performance *across companies*, even if they're financed or depreciate assets differently.

**What a declining Gross Margin might signal:** intense competition (price cuts/discounts), rising input costs without matching price increases, a shift toward lower-margin products, or falling volumes spreading fixed manufacturing costs over fewer units (raising per-unit cost).

**What a low Net Profit Margin might signal (even if Gross/EBITDA margins are healthy):** high interest burden, high tax expense, or heavy depreciation — i.e., the problem may lie *below* the operating line, not in core operations.

## 6.3 Worked Example — Full Margin Analysis

**Sunrise Foods Ltd.** (₹ Lakhs), FY 2021–22 and FY 2025–26:

| Particulars | 2021–22 | 2025–26 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 6,500 | 9,450 |
| Raw Materials | 3,250 | 4,470 |
| Employee Benefits | 940 | 1,250 |
| Manufacturing & Operating Exp. | 760 | 1,060 |
| S&A Expenses | 480 | 660 |
| Depreciation & Amortisation | 230 | 310 |
| Finance Costs | 110 | 160 |
| PBT | 730 | 1,540 |
| Tax | 185 | 390 |
| PAT | 545 | 1,150 |

**FY 2021–22:**
- Gross Profit = Revenue − Raw Materials = 6,500 − 3,250 = **4,250**; Gross Margin = 4,250/6,500 = **65.4%**
- EBITDA = Gross Profit − Employee Benefits − Manufacturing & Op. Exp. − S&A Exp. = 4,250 − 940 − 760 − 480 = **2,070**; EBITDA Margin = 2,070/6,500 = **31.8%**
- EBIT = EBITDA − Depreciation = 2,070 − 230 = **1,840**; EBIT Margin = 1,840/6,500 = **28.3%**
  *(Check: EBIT − Finance Cost = 1,840 − 110 = 1,730 ≈ PBT of 730? — Note: this simplified P&L already nets some items; always reconcile line items to the specific statement format given.)*
- Net Profit Margin = PAT/Revenue = 545/6,500 = **8.4%**

**FY 2025–26:**
- Gross Profit = 9,450 − 4,470 = **4,980**; Gross Margin = **52.7%**
- EBITDA = 4,980 − 1,250 − 1,060 − 660 = **2,010**; EBITDA Margin = **21.3%**
- EBIT = 2,010 − 310 = **1,700**; EBIT Margin = **18.0%**
- Net Profit Margin = 1,150/9,450 = **12.2%**

**Trend comment:** Even though absolute PAT grew (₹545 → ₹1,150 Lakhs), Gross and EBITDA *margins declined* (65.4%→52.7%, 31.8%→21.3%) — meaning revenue grew, but costs (especially raw materials) grew *faster* as a share of sales. This is a case where you must look at **margins, not just absolute profit**, to correctly judge whether profitability is truly improving.

# PART 7: PRACTICE FAQs WITH FULL SOLUTIONS

### Q1. (Depreciation – WDV) An asset costs $200,000, useful life 6 years, salvage $10,000. Find depreciation for the first 3 years (WDV) and accumulated depreciation.
**Solution:**
Rate = 1 − (Salvage/Cost)^(1/n) = 1 − (10,000/200,000)^(1/6) = 1 − (0.05)^0.1667 ≈ **39.79%**
- Year 1: 200,000 × 39.79% = **$79,580**; NBV = 120,420
- Year 2: 120,420 × 39.79% = **$47,923**; NBV = 72,497
- Year 3: 72,497 × 39.79% = **$28,847**; NBV = 43,650
**Accumulated depreciation after 3 years = 79,580+47,923+28,847 = $156,350**

### Q2. (Depreciation – Units of Production) Equipment costs $120,000, expected to produce 400,000 units. Produces 50,000/60,000/70,000 units in years 1–3. Find depreciation and accumulated depreciation.
**Solution:** Rate per unit = 120,000/400,000 = $0.30/unit
- Year 1: 50,000 × 0.30 = **$15,000**
- Year 2: 60,000 × 0.30 = **$18,000**
- Year 3: 70,000 × 0.30 = **$21,000**
**Accumulated depreciation = $54,000** (Note: if a residual value had been specified, we'd use (Cost − Residual)/400,000 as the per-unit rate instead.)

### Q3. (Depreciation – SLM vs WDV comparison) A vehicle costs $70,000, salvage $7,000, useful life 7 years. Compare total depreciation after 4 years under SLM vs WDV.
**Solution:**
**SLM:** Annual Dep = (70,000−7,000)/7 = $9,000/year → 4 years = **$36,000**

**WDV:** Rate = 1 − (7,000/70,000)^(1/7) = 1 − (0.1)^0.1429 ≈ 1 − 0.7197 ≈ **28.03%**
- Y1: 70,000×28.03% = 19,621; NBV=50,379
- Y2: 50,379×28.03% = 14,121; NBV=36,258
- Y3: 36,258×28.03% = 10,163; NBV=26,095
- Y4: 26,095×28.03% = 7,314; NBV=18,781
**Total WDV depreciation after 4 years = 19,621+14,121+10,163+7,314 ≈ $51,219**

**Comparison:** WDV front-loads depreciation ($51,219 in 4 years) vs. SLM's even spread ($36,000 in 4 years) — WDV reduces reported net income (and asset book value) faster in the earlier years.

### Q4. (Depreciation – choice of method impact on net income) An asset costs $90,000, useful life 9 years, salvage $9,000. How does WDV vs SLM affect net income in the first 4 years?
**Solution:**
**SLM:** (90,000−9,000)/9 = **$9,000/year** → constant expense of $9,000 each year for 4 years = $36,000 total.

**WDV:** Rate = 1−(9,000/90,000)^(1/9) = 1 − (0.1)^0.1111 ≈ 1 − 0.7743 ≈ **22.57%**
- Y1: 90,000×22.57%=20,313
- Y2: 69,687×22.57%=15,728
- Y3: 53,959×22.57%=12,178
- Y4: 41,781×22.57%=9,430
Total = 20,313+15,728+12,178+9,430 ≈ **$57,649**

**Impact on Net Income:** Since WDV charges more depreciation upfront ($57,649 vs $36,000 over 4 years), **net income will be lower under WDV in the early years** (higher expense) and correspondingly **higher under WDV in later years** (once SLM's steady expense overtakes WDV's shrinking charge) — total depreciation over the full useful life is identical either way; only the *timing* differs.

### Q5. (Transaction Analysis) Lalit started his business with capital of ₹40 Lakhs and some bank loan. He bought vehicles (₹8L, cash), building (₹26L, cash), inventory (₹4L — paid ₹1.5L, balance payable). Paid ₹1.2L for ads. Received an advance of ₹1.8L from a customer for goods (cost ₹1L) not yet delivered. Ends the week with ₹10.1L in bank. How much loan did he take?
**Solution:**
Cash inflows: Capital (40L) + Loan (L) + Customer advance (1.8L)
Cash outflows: Vehicles (8L) + Building (26L) + Inventory partial payment (1.5L) + Advertising (1.2L)
Closing cash = 10.1L

40 + L + 1.8 − 8 − 26 − 1.5 − 1.2 = 10.1
41.8 + L − 36.7 = 10.1
5.1 + L = 10.1
**L = ₹5 Lakhs (Bank Loan taken)**
*(Note: the customer advance is a liability (unearned revenue) since goods haven't been delivered — it does not count as revenue yet, but it IS a cash inflow, which is what matters for this cash-balance question.)*

### Q6. (Transaction Analysis) Preeti's firm sold inventory for ₹80 lakhs in May (70% collected in May). In June, sold ₹1.1 crore (60% collected in June), plus 20% of May's sales collected in June. Find Trade Receivables at end of June.
**Solution:**
May sales = 80L; collected in May = 56L (70%); outstanding after May = 24L
June: 20% of May's sales collected in June = 0.20 × 80L = 16L → remaining from May = 24 − 16 = **8L still outstanding from May**
June sales = 110L; collected in June (60%) = 66L → outstanding from June = 110 − 66 = **44L**
**Trade Receivables at end of June = 8L + 44L = ₹52 Lakhs**

### Q7. (Cash Flow – Operating Activities, Indirect-style via given data) Health Sports Company: Net income for the year = $28,000, including a $5,000 loss on sale of equipment and $6,000 depreciation expense. Compute Cash Flow from Operating Activities (ignoring working capital changes, which weren't provided).
**Solution:**
Start with Net Income and add back non-cash items / non-operating items:
Net Income $28,000
Add: Depreciation (non-cash expense) +$6,000
Add: Loss on sale of equipment (non-operating, added back since it's not a core operating cash outflow) +$5,000
**Cash Flow from Operating Activities = 28,000 + 6,000 + 5,000 = $39,000**
*(A "loss" on sale reduced net income on paper but didn't consume operating cash, so it's added back — just like a gain on sale would be subtracted, since it inflated net income without being an operating cash inflow.)*

### Q8. (Profitability – Depreciation as the plug) Skyline Hotels Ltd.: Revenue 45,00,000; COGS 24,00,000; Employee Exp. 6,00,000; Other Opex 3,60,000; Depreciation = ?; Finance Cost 1,20,000; PBT 4,20,000. Gross carrying amount of depreciable assets = 24,00,000, SLM method. Find (a) annual depreciation and (b) depreciation rate.
**Solution:**
Work down from Revenue to isolate Depreciation:
Revenue − COGS − Employee Exp − Other Opex − Depreciation − Finance Cost = PBT
45,00,000 − 24,00,000 − 6,00,000 − 3,60,000 − D − 1,20,000 = 4,20,000
10,20,000 − D = 4,20,000
**D = ₹6,00,000 (Annual Depreciation)**

**Depreciation Rate** = Annual Depreciation / Gross Carrying Amount = 6,00,000 / 24,00,000 = **25% per annum**

### Q9. (Cash Flow – Inventory Purchases under Cash Budget) Metro Sports Ltd.: COGS = 68% of sales. Sales: March ₹1.20 cr, April ₹1.40 cr, May ₹1.50 cr. Ending inventory = 30% of next month's COGS. Opening inventory (1 April) = ₹30 lakh. Find inventory purchases in April.
**Solution:**
April COGS = 0.68 × 1.40 cr = ₹95.2 lakh
April closing inventory = 30% of May's COGS = 0.30 × (0.68 × 1.50 cr) = 0.30 × 102 lakh = ₹30.6 lakh
April opening inventory = given = ₹30 lakh
**April Purchases = COGS + Closing Inv − Opening Inv = 95.2 + 30.6 − 30 = ₹95.8 lakh**

### Q10. (Integrated Working Capital) Harmony Pharmaceuticals Ltd., April: Credit sales ₹1.80 cr; Cash sales ₹60 lakh; Inventory purchased ₹1.50 cr; Paid suppliers ₹1.10 cr; Customers paid ₹1.40 cr; COGS ₹1.20 cr; Opening Receivables ₹30 lakh; Opening Payables ₹24 lakh; Opening Inventory ₹40 lakh. Find closing Receivables, Payables, and Inventory.
**Solution:**

= 30L + 180L − 140L = **₹70 Lakh**

**Closing Trade Payables** = Opening Payables + Purchases − Payments to Suppliers

# PART 8: QUICK-FIRE CONCEPT CHECK (Short FAQs)

**Q11.** What does the accounting equation state? — *Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity.*

**Q12.** Which concept requires revenue to be recorded when earned, not when cash is received? — *Accrual concept.*

**Q13.** Is land depreciated? — *No — it has an indefinite life (unless it's a quarry/landfill with limited life).*

**Q14.** Does depreciation stop if an asset's market value rises? — *No — depreciation is cost allocation, not valuation; it continues per the schedule regardless of market value.*

**Q15.** Which depreciation method charges the highest expense in the earliest years? — *Written Down Value (WDV) / Diminishing Balance Method.*

**Q16.** In a Cash Flow Statement (indirect method), do you add or subtract an increase in Trade Payables? — *Add — it means expenses were incurred but not yet paid in cash, so cash was preserved.*

**Q17.** In a Cash Flow Statement, do you add or subtract an increase in Inventory? — *Subtract — cash was used to build up stock.*

**Q18.** Which financial statement uses accrual accounting, and which uses cash accounting? — *Income Statement/Balance Sheet use accrual accounting; the Cash Flow Statement uses cash accounting.*

**Q19.** What's the difference between EBITDA and EBIT? — *EBITDA excludes Depreciation & Amortisation; EBIT is calculated after deducting Depreciation & Amortisation from EBITDA.*

**Q20.** Why might Net Profit Margin be low even when Gross Profit Margin is healthy? — *High interest expense, high tax burden, or heavy depreciation eating into profit below the operating-profit line.*

**Q21.** What is the formula for Cost of Goods Sold? — *COGS = Opening Inventory + Purchases − Closing Inventory.*

**Q22.** Under the Matching concept, how is rent expense treated versus raw material cost? — *Rent is a period cost matched to the period it relates to; raw material is a product cost matched to the units sold (via COGS).*

**Q23.** What three activities make up the Cash Flow Statement? — *Operating, Investing, and Financing activities.*

**Q24.** Why do we add back depreciation when computing Cash Flow from Operating Activities? — *Because depreciation reduced accounting profit but involved no actual cash outflow — it must be added back to arrive at real cash generated.*

**Q25.** What's the key difference between Amortization and Depreciation? — *They're conceptually identical cost-allocation processes; Depreciation applies to tangible assets, Amortization applies to intangible assets (usually via straight-line method).*
